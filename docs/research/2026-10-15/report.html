01 · The short answer

What the market looks like, and where Lockdown fits

  • The market has split in two. Free built-in managers (Apple, Google, Edge) are now good enough for plain logins. Paid managers win only on extras: more item types, sharing, every platform, developer tools, and business features. Prices rose in 2026: 1Password Individual is $47.88 a year, and Bitwarden Premium went from $10 to $19.80.
  • Switching is getting easy in both directions. A new standard, credential exchange, moves passwords and passkeys between apps on iOS 26 and Android (since June 2026). That lowers the cost of joining Lockdown. It lowers the cost of leaving too, so loyalty has to come from quality.
  • The open gaps are trust, reliability, and recovery. A 2026 ETH Zurich study imagined the company's own server turning hostile. It found 12 attacks on Bitwarden, 7 on LastPass, 6 on Dashlane, and 2 on 1Password. Autofill that misses fields is the top complaint about every product.
  • Some things nobody ships well yet. Post-quantum protection for shared vault keys. Vault history you can verify. An audit log for AI-agent access from day one. Approving a TV sign-in from your phone. One place that knows every way you sign in to each site.
  • Your vendor-login idea is right in spirit, and the mechanism should mostly be standards. Copying each vendor's private phone app is legally risky, fragile, and makes a breach worse. Passkeys, one-time codes, and TV activation codes give the same "scan and approve" experience. Steam is the one private bridge worth building, as a fenced, opt-in feature.
  • Build it on one Rust core with thin native shells. SwiftUI on Apple, Jetpack Compose on Android, and one web UI for the extension, web vault, and desktop. One token system feeds all three.
  • Plan for a small team. A credible v1 is about 12 to 18 months with 6 to 8 engineers. It matches the incumbents on basics and gets the cryptography right from day one.

02 · Your vendor-login idea

Steam, Battle.net, and Adobe: what's feasible

The goal of signing people in through each service's own system, and replacing their separate authenticator apps, is achievable. For most vendors, the way there is a passkey or a code, not an imitation of their phone app.

Steam Build · fenced

QR sign-in approval works today in open-source tools. Steam's QR holds a link like s.team/q/1/…. Approving it means signing a small message with the account's shared_secret. Valve has tolerated third-party authenticators for about 8 years.

Codes and file import in v1. QR approval and trade confirmations in v1.x, opt-in, never auto-confirm.

Battle.net Use passkeys

Nobody has publicly rebuilt Blizzard's "Approve this login" push. Pulling out the code secret still works through a private web flow, but it calls Blizzard's private API. Battle.net now appears in the passkey directory.

Passkeys in v1. Accept code secrets users bring from other tools in v1.x. No in-app extractor. Decline the push.

Adobe Use passkeys

Adobe supports passkeys and FIDO2 security keys (help page updated February 2026). Community reports say Adobe removed the third-party authenticator-app option in late 2025. Nobody has rebuilt the Adobe Account Access push.

Passkeys in v1. Decline the push.

Three different things hide inside one idea

1 · One-time codes

Safe and expected

The site gives you a shared secret once, by QR. The app turns it into a new code every 30 seconds (TOTP, RFC 6238). Steam uses a 5-character variant.

2 · Being the vendor's phone app

Where the risk lives

Approving a vendor's private push or QR means speaking their private interface. That is usually reverse-engineered, usually against their terms, and means storing far more dangerous secrets.

Steam only
3 · Standards-based sign-in

The real answer

Passkey "hybrid" sign-in (laptop shows a QR, phone approves) and TV device codes (RFC 8628) are open standards built for exactly this.

Build · centerpiece

The centerpiece: being a passkey provider

On iPhone, every enabled credential provider gets the "scan the QR on your laptop, approve on your phone" flow for free from iOS. An Apple engineer confirmed this in the developer forums in June 2026. On Android 14 and later, third-party providers can answer a desktop QR too. Bitwarden, 1Password, and Thales document it, though Google's own docs don't say so directly, and a few manufacturers' devices lack support.

So one integration covers Adobe, Battle.net, PlayStation, Nintendo, Microsoft, Google, GitHub, X, and every other passkey site. No per-vendor reverse engineering is needed.

The generalization: point your phone at the TV

TVs and consoles already use an open standard (RFC 8628) for "approve this sign-in from another device." Lockdown acts as the approving browser. It touches nothing private and behaves exactly like a person typing.

  1. ScanThe TV shows a QR with the activation address and code built in.
  2. OpenLockdown opens the service's real page, from an allow-list, in its own secure browser.
  3. FillLockdown fills the username, password, and one-time code.
  4. ApproveLockdown shows which service, account, and device. The user taps approve.
The hard safety rule

Device-code sign-in is one of the most-abused phishing patterns of 2025 and 2026. Microsoft calls it "a high-risk authentication method." Lockdown approves a code only when the user scans it from a screen in front of them. It refuses codes that arrive by message, email, link, or paste. No setting can override this.

Known activation pages: microsoft.com/link, youtube.com/activate, epicgames.com/id/activate, discord.com/activate, netflix.com/tv8, disneyplus.com/begin, amazon.com/code.

Bridge manifest and crown-jewel storage rules

Each bridge ships a signed manifest. The UI draws itself from it, so Steam shows a "Confirm trade" control and Epic does not.

id: steam
tier: tolerated            # official | tolerated (closed bridges are never built)
capabilities: [generate_code, approve_qr_login, confirm_transaction, import, transfer, enroll]
secrets:
  shared_secret:   { class: high }
  steam_session:   { class: high }
  identity_secret: { class: crown_jewel }
  revocation_code: { class: crown_jewel, display_only: true }
disclosures: [tos_risk, trade_hold_by_path, one_authenticator_per_account]
requires_proximity_gate: [approve_qr_login]
kill_switch: true
  • Crown-jewel secrets (Steam identity_secret, crypto recovery phrases, your email account's master login) get their own separately wrapped key.
  • Signing a confirmation needs a fresh biometric check and a deliberate tap. Revealing or exporting needs the full account password.
  • They are never autofilled, never shown in search previews, never given to AI agents, and left out of bulk exports unless the user opts in. "This device only" is an option.
  • Every imported file is treated as hostile input. Fake Steam Desktop Authenticator builds stole accounts in 2018.
  • Legal: get US and EU counsel before shipping the Steam bridge, about the Steam Subscriber Agreement and anti-circumvention law (DMCA §1201). This research is not legal advice.

06 · Security architecture

Assume our own server turns hostile

Design as if Lockdown's server actively lies to the apps: swapping keys, replaying old data, dropping items, or tampering with fields. The apps must catch it. That is the exact threat model the ETH Zurich and USI team used (USENIX Security 2026). It is also what a real breach or a coerced insider looks like.

The key tree: each key locks the next one down

Account unlock keyFrom your password (stretched with Argon2id), the login protocol's output, and the Secret Key.
User keyRandom. Can also be opened by "slots": biometrics in the Secure Enclave or TPM, a passkey (PRF), or the recovery kit.
Identity keysA hybrid classic-plus-post-quantum key pair for receiving shares, and a signing key pair.
Vault keysOne random 256-bit key per vault, shared by wrapping it to members' identity keys.
Item keysOne per item, so sharing or revoking one item doesn't re-encrypt everything.
FieldsAuthenticated encryption glued to the item, vault, field name, and format version, so a server can't move a password into another field.

Login with OPAQUE, not SRP

OPAQUE (RFC 9807, July 2025) proves you know your password without sending it, and never reveals the salt, so attackers can't pre-compute guesses. WhatsApp uses it in production. Argon2id runs on the device at 64 MiB or more, and apps refuse server-sent settings below a floor.

Others Bitwarden sends a derived hash, not a PAKE. 1Password and Proton use the older SRP.

Secret Key on by default

A random 128-bit code mixed into the unlock key. Stolen server data becomes useless for password guessing. New devices get it by scanning a QR on an old device, so it's rarely typed.

Others 1Password has the strongest version, but users get locked out when resets go wrong. Lockdown pairs it with a recovery drill.

Post-quantum key wrapping

Vault encryption already resists quantum computers. Shared keys don't: an attacker can copy them now and decrypt later. Lockdown wraps every vault, family, and recovery key with a hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768 scheme from day one.

Others 1Password and Keeper use post-quantum only for the network connection. Bitwarden still wraps shares with RSA-OAEP (SHA-1). No major manager does vault-level hybrid wrapping, as far as we can tell.

Key transparency

A public, append-only log of everyone's public keys. If the server shows you a fake key for your sister, her app notices. High-value shares add an in-person QR check.

Others In the ETH study, Bitwarden, LastPass, and 1Password sharing trusted the server's word for public keys. Proton is the exception.

Anti-rollback manifests

Each vault keeps a signed, hash-chained table of contents. A server can't hide an item or serve an old version unnoticed. The same chain powers visible item history: "Dad changed the Netflix password on 3 October."

Others No mainstream manager offers verifiable vault history.

Clickjack-proof autofill

Menus sit in the browser's top layer, the whole page structure is checked for hidden overlays, and codes, cards, and passkeys need a second deliberate action, by default.

Others At DEF CON 33, 10 of 11 managers leaked credentials with one click. 1Password's fix is an opt-in prompt that is off by default.

07 · Tech stack and design system

One Rust core, thin native shells, one token source

  • Rust core for crypto, the vault model, sync, credential exchange, the address matcher, the code engine, the approval service, and the bridge host. Bitwarden, Proton, and 1Password all moved their cores to Rust.
  • Bindings: UniFFI generates Swift and Kotlin; wasm-bindgen compiles the core for the extension and web vault.
  • Mobile: native SwiftUI and Jetpack Compose. Autofill extensions, Credential Manager, key stores, and the camera all need native code.
  • Browser extension: Manifest V3 for Chrome, Edge, and Firefox, plus a Safari Web Extension. No long-lived secrets in the background worker.
  • Server: one Rust binary (axum, sqlx) with SQLite or Postgres. Cloud and self-hosted run the same code, so security fixes ship to both at once.
Desktop shellForAgainst
Tauri 2 RecommendedSmall and fast. Talks to the Rust core natively. Smaller attack surface. Reuses the web UI.Three web engines to test (WebKit on Mac, Chromium on Windows, WebKitGTK on Linux). MSIX packaging for the Windows passkey plug-in is unproven.
ElectronLowest risk; one engine everywhere.Heavy, a big npm supply chain, and a long-running complaint about 1Password.
Fully nativeBest OS feel.About three times the cost. Too much for a small team.

What this means for the design system

  • One token source. Figma variables → DTCG JSON → generated Swift, Kotlin, and CSS. Nobody hand-edits platform files.
  • Three tiers: global, semantic, and component, with modes for light, dark, and increased contrast, plus a density setting for desktop versus touch.
  • Type follows the platform: semantic roles map to Dynamic Type, Android font scale, and rem.
  • One spec, three builds. Each component has one written spec and three implementations, linked with Code Connect and screenshot-tested per platform and engine.
  • Security components belong in the system: an approval sheet with number matching, risk-tier badges, a secret field with a clipboard timer, a code display for 6-digit, 8-digit, and Steam codes, a TV large-type view, and crown-jewel styling that web content can't imitate.
  • Surfaces you can't style: the iOS keyboard bar, system passkey sheets, Windows Hello, and browser passkey dialogs. Design hand-offs to them and document them.
  • Clickjacking is a design constraint. In-page menus are always opaque, have a minimum size, and sit in the top layer. Sensitive approvals happen in the app or OS.

08 · Roadmap

Four phases for a team of 6 to 8 engineers

Assumes two engineers on the Rust core and crypto, one iOS, one Android, two on web, extension, and desktop, and one on the server. Plus a design lead, a product lead, and a contracted auditor and cryptographer.

v1 · about 12–18 months

Trustworthy parity

Move from 1Password, Bitwarden, or Apple Passwords in under 10 minutes and lose nothing used daily. The cryptography is final-grade from day one.

  • iOS, Android, all four browser extensions, Tauri desktop for Mac and Windows, web vault
  • Full key tree, OPAQUE, Secret Key, post-quantum wrapping, key transparency, signed manifests
  • Passkey provider with automatic upgrades; codes including Steam; Steam file import
  • Credential exchange plus importers from every major manager; free export forever
  • Breach checks, three breach playbooks, the "sign in with" map
  • Recovery kit, device approval, trusted contacts, recovery drill; self-hosting

Exit: audit with no open high findings; 95% autofill success on a 500-site test set; lossless round-trip imports; 9 of 10 test users recover from the kit alone.

v1.x · about 6–9 months later

The sign-in hub

Your vendor-login vision, through legitimate means.

  • TV and console activation with the proximity rule
  • Steam bridge: QR approval and trade confirmations, opt-in, with a kill switch
  • Battle.net and other code imports
  • Windows passkey plug-in; Linux desktop app
  • SSH agent with Git signing; CLI with scoped tokens
  • Passkey (PRF) unlock; digital legacy; visible item history
  • Android SMS codes relayed to desktop; panic lock; family roles

Exit: TV activation works on 5 of 7 services and passes a red-team phishing test; Steam bridge runs 60 days with no auto-confirm path.

v2 · about 9–12 months later

Teams and agents

Business revenue, and leading on safe AI-agent access.

  • SSO without a master password, SCIM, policies, multi-admin recovery
  • "Rotate on revoke": flag every item a departing member could see
  • Agent credential broker over MCP: actions not getters, scoped tokens, per-task approval, an encrypted audit log on day one
  • Watch approvals; on-device email codes; document scanning
  • A second independent audit

Exit: 5 pilot organizations onboard without engineering help; a red team can't extract a secret through the agent broker.

Later

When the standards settle

  • Agentic password change (Apple announced it for iOS 27, then delayed it past 27.0)
  • Cross-device credential exchange (CXP) once the protocol stabilizes
  • First-party email aliases
  • Short-lived SSH certificates; collaborative notes; a binary-transparency log
Never

Things we won't build

  • Hidden vaults that pretend to be empty. Sync traffic leaks them, and a coercer is never satisfied. Ship the visible panic lock instead.
  • Emulating private vendor push channels
  • Reading Gmail on Lockdown's servers
  • Auto-following email magic links
  • Auto-confirming Steam trades

If v1 slips, cut in this order: web vault editing (keep read-only), share links to non-users, the third breach playbook, Windows desktop, then self-hosting at launch. Never cut the cryptography, the audit, credential-exchange import, or autofill quality.

Product and business risks

  • Autofill quality decides everything. Budget a dedicated autofill engineer and the site test set from the start.
  • A new brand must earn trust. One serious bug early would hurt badly.
  • Free built-ins keep improving. Apple's delayed password changer will likely ship in a later iOS 27 update.
  • Approval fatigue. Every prompt teaches people to tap yes. Approve per task, and use number matching.
  • Platform policy. Apple's export rules are undocumented; Google Play restricts accessibility autofill and SMS access.
  • Succession. Raivo was sold and codes vanished; Buttercup was abandoned. Publish a commitment: free export forever, and no custody change without consent.
